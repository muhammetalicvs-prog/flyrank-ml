Can Search and Engagement Signals Prioritize Content for Human Review?

An honest comparison of a transparent fixed-rule baseline, Logistic Regression, and Random Forest on a client-grouped holdout.

Author: Muhammet Ali Çavuş Primary metric: Precision@50 Test records: 6,163
Abstract Problem Data Methodology Results Limitations Recommendations Reproducibility Credit

Abstract

This study asks whether historical search visibility, click-through, engagement, and content-update signals can be used to prioritize content records for human review. Using a public-safe anonymized dataset from the FlyRank ML Internship, I compared an interpretable fixed-rule baseline with Logistic Regression and Random Forest on a client-grouped holdout split. The methods were evaluated primarily with Precision@50 because the operational objective is to place useful candidates near the top of a limited review queue. The fixed-rule baseline achieved the strongest Precision@50, while Random Forest achieved the highest ROC-AUC, showing that broader discrimination and top-of-queue usefulness can lead to different model choices. The results support using an interpretable ranked queue as a human decision aid, but they do not prove that acting on the recommendations will improve business performance.

Introduction / Problem Statement

Content teams often have more pages to inspect than reviewers can examine. The decision is therefore not whether every page should be changed, but which records deserve attention first.

This research tests whether historical search and engagement signals can produce a useful ranked review queue. The output is a prioritization aid for editors and SEO reviewers; it does not automatically edit, remove, or publish content.

Research question: Can public-safe historical signals rank content records that are more likely to require human review?

Data

The project uses the public-safe, anonymized dataset supplied through the FlyRank ML Internship. The release was derived from approximately 79 million rows of production search and analytics data. Modeling and ranking used the prepared content_refresh_anonymized.csv table containing 30,000 content-level records and 44 columns.

Signals include impressions, clicks, click-through rate, average search position, engagement measures, content freshness, and historical trend information. The forward-looking data-contract experiment used March 2026 as the feature window and April 2026 as the outcome window.

Public-safety and leakage exclusions

Client and content identifiers were retained only for grouping or traceability and were not used as model features. Current- and previous-period fields used to construct the label, along with trend_direction and trend_pct, were excluded because they directly reveal or closely approximate the target. No client names, page URLs, private search queries, credentials, or identifying records appear in this page.

Methodology

Target and assumptions

The target is a binary decline indicator derived from a future outcome period. A positive label identifies a record that declined under the project definition. Historical visibility, engagement, freshness, and performance variables were treated as prioritization signals, not as proof that an edit would cause improvement.

Baseline and models

The Week 4 baseline uses a transparent action score combining visible search demand, search position, weak click-through performance, and related opportunity signals. It was compared with Logistic Regression and Random Forest probability rankings.

Validation design

The split was grouped by client rather than created by random row. Training contained 23,837 records from 25 clients; testing contained 6,163 records from 7 unseen clients, with zero client overlap. This design tests transfer to unseen client groups and limits optimistic leakage through repeated client patterns.

Evaluation

Precision@50 is the primary operational metric because the review process focuses on a small queue. Precision@20, Recall@50, Average Precision, ROC-AUC, and the test base rate are reported as supporting measures.

The baseline’s Precision@50 of 0.88 means that 44 of its first 50 recommendations were positive cases on the held-out split. Logistic Regression reached 0.64 and Random Forest 0.58. Random Forest’s higher ROC-AUC reflects broader discrimination across the test set, but it did not produce the strongest small review queue.

Decision: retain the fixed-rule baseline as the initial queue generator for this specific dataset, label, split, and review capacity.

Limitations & Honest Framing

This is an offline evaluation. It does not measure what happens after editors act and does not prove that changing a recommended page will improve traffic, engagement, or business outcomes.

The label is a proxy for future decline rather than a direct measure of refresh value. Seasonality, search demand, competition, SERP changes, technical issues, and other unobserved factors may influence the outcome.

The baseline weights and thresholds were manually designed, the machine learning models were not exhaustively tuned, and the findings come from one prepared dataset and one grouped holdout. Performance may differ across future releases, markets, time periods, or review capacities.

Ranked Recommendations

1. Review title and meta information

Prioritize pages with meaningful visibility but weak click-through. Compare the title and description with likely search intent, the page promise, and competing SERP features.

2. Refresh visible but aging content

Inspect older pages that still receive impressions. Verify dates, examples, screenshots, and whether newer competing content answers the need more effectively.

3. Investigate engagement weakness

For records with visibility or clicks but weak engagement, review content-message alignment, readability, internal links, calls to action, performance, and layout issues.

4. Reassess low-confidence candidates

Keep low-volume and borderline records lower in the queue. Gather more observations and consider seasonality and intervention cost before acting.

Operationally, review the top 50 records, document the action and outcome, and use those post-review observations to recalibrate thresholds and improve future labels.
